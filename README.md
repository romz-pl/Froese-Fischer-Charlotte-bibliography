# Charlotte Froese Fischer


**Charlotte Froese Fischer (September 21, 1929 – February 8, 2024)** was a Canadian-American applied mathematician and computer scientist. She is best known for developing and implementing the multiconfiguration Hartree–Fock (MCHF) method for calculating atomic structure, and she is often called the "first lady of computational atomic structure theory."

**Early life and education**

She was born in the village of Pravdivka (formerly Nikolayevka) in the Donetsk region of what is now Ukraine, to parents of Mennonite descent. Shortly after her birth, her family fled and spent several months in a German refugee camp before emigrating to Canada. She excelled at school and won a scholarship to the University of British Columbia (UBC), where she earned a bachelor's degree in mathematics and chemistry in 1952 and a master's in chemistry in 1954. Her early research experience included a summer in 1954 at the Radio Physics Division of Canada's National Research Council, where she ran calculations about radio waves on a desk calculator. She then completed her PhD in 1957 under the British physicist Douglas Hartree, whose methods for solving the equations of atomic structure became the foundation of her later work.

**Academic career**

After her PhD she returned to UBC in 1957 as a professor in the Department of Mathematics, where she spent roughly a decade. In 1963 she became the first woman scientist to receive an Alfred P. Sloan Foundation Research Fellowship, and in 1963–64 she spent a year at the Harvard College Observatory, extending her atomic-structure work. She also worked at Boeing near Seattle for a time.

In 1967 she married Patrick C. Fischer, a mathematician and computer scientist. The couple moved to the University of Waterloo in Ontario, which was rapidly expanding its computer science program. There she was a professor of applied analysis and computer science from 1968 to 1975, introduced computer courses into the curriculum, and helped form the university's Computer Science Department. She then taught at Pennsylvania State University from 1974 to 1979. In 1980 she joined Vanderbilt University, where she became a research professor of computer science and was later named emerita, a title she held from 1996.

For roughly twenty years she was also a guest researcher in the Atomic Spectroscopy Group at the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in the United States. She had a break from NIST when she returned to Canada as an affiliate professor at UBC.

**Scientific contributions**

Her central achievement was the MCHF method. The Hartree–Fock approach approximates the behavior of electrons in atoms, but for many atoms and ions it is not accurate enough. MCHF improves on it by using a combination of configurations, meaning several possible arrangements of electrons, to describe the atomic state more precisely. She wrote the software to implement this method, which made detailed atomic calculations practical for researchers around the world. Work in this area supports spectroscopy, astrophysics, plasma and fusion research, and atomic data needed by many other fields.

She wrote influential textbooks on the subject and a scientific biography of her mentor, Douglas Hartree. By most measures her influence was large: Wikipedia notes that she is the most highly cited author in computational atomic physics, with more than 22,000 citations. Her career spanned several decades and touched on physics, mathematics, and computer science, which was unusual for the period.

**Honors and recognition**

She was elected a Fellow of the American Physical Society in 1991, and she was a foreign member of the Lithuanian Academy of Sciences from 2004. Her 1963 Sloan Fellowship was an early landmark for women in physical science.

**Later life and legacy**

She was married to Patrick Fischer until his death in 2011. Together they established the Patrick and Charlotte Fischer Computer Science Scholarship at Vanderbilt, which supports undergraduate study, and after his death she endowed the Patrick C. Fischer chair in theoretical computer science. She also created a scholarship fund at Vanderbilt and supported charitable causes related to education, nature, women, and development. She is survived by their daughter, Carolyn Fischer, an environmental economist.

Colleagues describe her as generous, kind, and collegial as well as dedicated. Her obituaries in *Physics in Canada* and in university publications portray her as a trailblazer whose work on atomic structure remained in use for decades after she first developed it.
